Systematic characterization of gesture stimuli is important for vocabulary-learning research, but human annotation is slow and costly to scale. Video-language models (VLMs) may support this process, yet their correspondence with human judgment requires empirical evaluation. We benchmarked twelve configurations from four model families (Google Gemini, OpenAI GPT, Alibaba Qwen, Elorian Glimpse) against 230 human raters across four language cohorts, who rated 148 instructional gesture–word pairings derived from 134 underlying clips on seven dimensions drawn from the Gesture Memory Support Model. Mean absolute error across configurations ranged from 0.53 to 1.12 points on five-point scales, and pooled rank correlations ranged from .62 to .69. Crucially, error and rank-order agreement dissociated: dimensions with the lowest error (enactment potential, motional salience) had the weakest rank correlations, while iconicity showed the opposite pattern. This dissociation mirrored a structural feature of the human data itself, where the same two dimensions showed the lowest interrater reliability and most compressed rating distributions. Models rated mismatched gesture–word pairs lower than matched pairs, indicating genuine sensitivity to semantic compatibility, though residual ratings remained above the human floor. Cultural familiarity showed the largest model–human discrepancies; independently, human ratings on this and related dimensions varied systematically by language cohort even after controlling for item identity, indicating that the human reference itself is not culturally neutral. These findings support selective, dimension-specific use of VLMs for gesture annotation under human oversight, rather than uniform deployment across the full rating profile. The benchmark establishes correspondence with human judgment; it does not establish whether model- or human-derived ratings predict vocabulary-learning outcomes, which remains an open empirical question.
